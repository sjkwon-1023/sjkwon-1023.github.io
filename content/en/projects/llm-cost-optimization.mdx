The first problem I set out to solve was evaluation consistency, not cost. When a generative LLM serves as the
judge, its response format and decision can vary even under the same criteria. Large batches therefore needed a
repeatable method of automated evaluation.

Cost still mattered. LLM spending on batch generation of English questions had reached roughly KRW 20 million
(about US$15,000), and post-generation validation with a high-end model cost roughly KRW 50 (US$0.04) and took
30 seconds per item. This PoC, however, targeted the per-item validation cost—not the entire accumulated
generation cost.

## A lower-cost commercial model instead of a local small language model

Reliable access to local GPU resources was difficult at the time, so I excluded self-hosting a small language
model (SLM). Instead, I
considered lower-cost commercial API models that could be compared directly within the existing pipeline. The
cost figures below are estimates calculated from the sample's input and output token usage and the API pricing at
the time, not production invoices.

## Changing the decision method before changing the model

Simply asking a lower-cost model for the same free-form evaluation would not make it easy to compare both cost
and decision stability. Instead of having the validation model generate an explanation, I built logic that
compared the log probabilities of candidate labels. The task needed a pass/fail decision, not a long answer.

I compared the lower-cost commercial model's decisions with those of the existing high-end model, examining
cost, processing time, agreement, and reproducibility across repeated runs. To hold conditions constant, I reran
the experiment within the Prefect-based generation, validation, and loading pipeline.

## PoC results

| | Existing judge | PoC candidate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Estimated token cost per item | Approx. KRW 50 (US$0.04) | Approx. KRW 5 (US$0.004; −90%) |
| Processing time per item | Approx. 30 sec | Approx. 15 sec (−50%) |

Within the PoC sample, every decision matched the existing high-end model. This was agreement with the incumbent
model, not accuracy measured against human-annotated ground truth. Within that scope, the experiment showed the
potential to separate validation into a cheaper model and a shorter classification task.

## What the PoC did not establish

Although every decision matched within the sample, this alone does not show that quality would hold across other
item types or larger batches. The project schedule did not allow expansion to type-stratified samples or
validation under production conditions, so the approach was not deployed in the live pipeline. This result is
therefore not a realized cost saving; it is a PoC that suggested a lower-cost commercial model might be viable
and narrowed the scope of the next validation study.
