The team began with the idea of having a 3D avatar express spoken or written Korean in Korean Sign
Language (KSL). The task was not initially defined as a translation problem. After analyzing AI
Hub data and the structures of Korean and sign-language morphemes, I defined the
Korean-to-sign-morpheme stage as a translation task and proposed starting with the BART family.

Our available environment was a Windows PC with 8 GB of VRAM. On the three-person team, I handled
EDA and translation-model fine-tuning. Later, I used Blender to diagnose the cause of errors in
the Unity animation.

## Defining the problem as translation and choosing BART

The target output was not a free-form Korean sentence but a short sequence of sign-language
morphemes. Given the 8 GB VRAM constraint and roughly 130,000 training examples, we chose the
encoder-decoder architecture of koBART base over a larger generative model. Rather than begin with
a model already fine-tuned for summarization or translation, I judged it better to teach the base
model the sign-language output format directly from our data.

Most of the original data consisted of sentences describing disaster scenarios. We used the
sign-language morphemes to prompt an LLM to generate everyday Korean sentences, expanding the
training set to roughly 130,000 examples, although the generated sentences were repetitive. We
also considered using a separate Korean everyday-conversation dataset as input, but excluded it
because of storage constraints.

I tested `gogamza/kobart-base-v2` and `hyunwoongko/kobart`. Because
`gogamza/kobart-base-v2` continued to repeat the same output across parameter changes, I excluded
it. I selected and fine-tuned `hyunwoongko/kobart`, whose output was stable under the default
configuration. During these experiments, extending the input and output lengths to accommodate a
small number of long samples worsened validation loss, so I kept the maximum length at 128.

In a ten-epoch run, validation loss began rising again after roughly the fifth epoch. I therefore
fixed later experiments at five epochs and saved the checkpoint with the lowest validation loss.

![Training and validation loss curves for hyunwoongko/kobart](/media/sign-language/training-loss.png)

Another issue was that sign-language morphemes combining Korean characters and numbers, such as
`먼지1#`, did not map one-to-one to existing tokenizer tokens. Instead of adding a
morpheme-specific vocabulary and retraining the tokenizer, I constrained decoding so the model
could generate only token sequences corresponding to morphemes found in the dataset while
preserving the meaning carried by Korean subword units the tokenizer already knew.

## Finding why the coordinates broke

For each translated morpheme, we looked up the corresponding facial, pose, and both-hand
coordinates in the source JSON and converted them into frame sequences. The image below shows the
source sign-language video with facial, pose, and both-hand keypoint annotations.

By isolating and comparing those stages, I found that the Armature assumed by the coordinate data
had a different number of bones from the downloaded rig, breaking the mapping. I documented the
reproduction steps and root cause in a bug report. The teammate responsible for Unity then used
that diagnosis to switch to another rigged model compatible with the coordinate structure and
complete the final character animation.

## What we implemented—and what remains unvalidated

After changing the model and constraining its output, the system produced examples like the
following. Because the model operates on Korean, the original input and morpheme output are
preserved alongside an English gloss.

> Korean input: `내일 오후 미세먼지 주의보가 발령되었으니 외부활동을 삼가해 주시기 바랍니다`
> English gloss: “A fine-dust advisory has been issued for tomorrow afternoon, so please refrain

translation-quality result.
